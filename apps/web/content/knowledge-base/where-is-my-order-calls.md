A "where is my order" call comes from a customer who has paid for something and wants to know when it will arrive. Retailers often shorten it to WISMO. An AI voice agent can answer most of these calls on its own: it confirms who is calling, looks the order up in your store's order and shipping systems, explains the status in plain words, sends the tracking link and passes anything unusual to a person.

## Why order status calls pile up

Buying online opens a gap between paying and receiving, and every day in that gap is a reason to pick up the phone. The usual triggers are easy to spot:

- **Tracking pages are hard to read.** "Label created", "in transit" and "exception" mean something to a warehouse. To a shopper they raise more questions.
- **The shipping email went missing.** It landed in spam, went to an old address, or the shopper checked out as a guest and has no account to log in to.
- **Something is late.** A backorder, a weather delay, a holiday rush or a split shipment where half the order arrived.
- **The buyer isn't the recipient.** Gift orders bring calls from people who never saw the receipt.
- **Peaks stack up.** The days after a sale and the weeks before a holiday bring the most orders, and a few days later, the most calls about them.

It shows up across retail: a dress needed for Saturday, a sofa delivery window, a birthday gift that cannot arrive late. The calls are routine, and they still matter. The caller has already paid, so a slow or vague answer costs trust.

## Measure your own volume first

Before you change anything, find out how many of your calls are about order status. Read a week of call notes or transcripts and tag each one by reason: order status, cancel or change, return or exchange, product question, everything else.

Here is the arithmetic, as an example. Say your store gets 60 calls a day, and 25 of them are about order status. At about 3 minutes each, that is 25 × 3 = 75 minutes a day of someone reading tracking pages aloud. Over a 30-day month, it comes to about 37 hours. Your numbers will differ, so use your own.

Note when the calls arrive, too. Our guide to [missed calls](/knowledge-base/missed-calls) shows how to read your call logs for those patterns.

## What callers actually want

### Has it shipped?

Most order systems move an order through a handful of stages: placed, processing, packed, shipped, out for delivery, delivered. Some add partially shipped, backordered or cancelled. The agent's job is to say which stage the order is in, in words a shopper uses: "It was packed yesterday and is waiting for the carrier to collect it."

Split shipments need extra care. "Two of your three items shipped on Tuesday. The jacket ships separately, and I'll send that tracking link too once it has one."

### Where is it now?

The latest tracking scan, translated. "Label created" means the parcel exists in the carrier's system but hasn't been picked up yet. "Exception" can mean a missed delivery, a damaged label or a weather hold, so the agent should read the detail behind it before answering.

### When will it arrive?

Give the estimated delivery date from the shipping data and call it an estimate. The agent should not promise a date the carrier hasn't given. If the caller needs it by a fixed date, such as a wedding or a birthday, it should say plainly whether that looks likely and what the options are.

### Why is it late?

Callers handle bad news better than no news. Say what caused the delay if you know it (a backorder, a carrier delay, an address problem), what your store will do about it and when the caller should hear next.

### It says delivered, but I don't have it

Your store needs a written rule for this, and the agent should follow it word for word. A common pattern: check around the door, with neighbors and any mailroom, then wait a set period, since parcels are sometimes scanned as delivered a little early. If it still hasn't turned up, open a case with your team. The agent can take the details and set the expectation. A person decides on the replacement or refund.

### It arrived damaged or wrong

The agent takes the order number, the item and what went wrong, then starts your damage or wrong-item process. That often leads to a return or exchange, which we cover in [returns and exchanges by phone](/insights/returns-and-exchanges-by-phone).

### Can I still change it?

Many order status calls turn into change requests: a wrong size, a typo in the address, a faster shipping speed. If the order hasn't shipped yet, these are often quick to handle. See [order cancellations and changes by phone](/insights/order-cancellations-and-changes-by-phone) for how to set the rules.

## How the agent verifies the caller

An order record holds a name, an address, a phone number and a list of what someone bought. None of that should go to whoever happens to call. A simple check protects your customers without slowing honest callers down.

1. **Ask for the order number.** If the caller doesn't have it, the agent can search by the email address or phone number on the order instead.
2. **Ask for a second detail that must match.** The email address, phone number, or billing or shipping zip code on the order.
3. **Treat caller ID as a hint.** A call from the phone number on the order helps the agent find it faster. Caller ID can be faked, so it shouldn't count as proof on its own.
4. **Share only what the call needs.** Status, items and dates are fine once the caller is verified. Confirm an address by its last few characters ("the address ending in Elm Street, apartment 4") instead of reading it out in full. Card details never come up.
5. **Stop after two misses.** If the details don't match twice, the agent offers general information only and passes the call to a person or books a callback.

Gift orders need their own rule. A recipient may have a tracking number and nothing else. Many stores let the agent confirm the delivery status and date to a recipient while keeping the price and the buyer's details private.

## How the agent answers from your systems

A voice agent is only as useful as what it can look up. On a where-is-my-order call, it typically checks four things in your store's order and shipping systems: the order record, its fulfillment status, the tracking events and the estimated delivery date. Our explainer on [how voice AI works](/knowledge-base/how-voice-ai-works) covers how those lookups happen in the middle of a conversation.

Here is what a short call can sound like:

> **Caller:** Hi, I ordered a jacket last week and it hasn't come.
>
> **Agent:** I can check that for you. What's the order number?
>
> **Caller:** It's 4-8-2-1-7.
>
> **Agent:** Thanks. And the zip code it's shipping to?
>
> **Caller:** 60614.
>
> **Agent:** Got it. Your jacket shipped on Monday and the latest scan shows it at the regional facility. The estimated delivery is Thursday. Would you like the tracking link by text?

The answer is only as fresh as your data. If your warehouse marks orders as shipped once a day, the agent will be up to a day behind, so tell it what to say when the data is thin: "I don't see a tracking number yet. Orders usually ship within two business days, and you'll get an email with tracking as soon as it does." Use your store's real shipping times.

## Sending the tracking link

A link beats reading a long tracking number aloud. Have the agent offer it at the end of every status call, by text or email.

- **Send it only to contact details already on the order.** A request to send order details to a new number or address is a common trick, and the agent should decline it.
- **Get consent for texts.** In the US, business texting generally requires registering your number for application-to-person messaging, and every text should include a way to opt out.
- **Keep it short.** The order number, the status in one line and the link.

## When to hand the call to a person

Good automation knows when to stop. Set the agent to transfer, or to open a case and book a callback, when:

- the caller asks for a person
- a parcel shows as delivered and still hasn't turned up after your waiting period
- the item arrived damaged or wrong and your rules say a person decides the fix
- the caller fails verification, or something about the request looks like fraud
- the order has already shipped and the caller needs the address changed
- the caller is upset, or has called about the same order more than once
- the order is above a value you set
- the request falls outside the rules you wrote

A warm transfer passes the caller along with a short summary, so your team doesn't start from zero. If nobody is free, the agent should log the case and tell the caller when to expect a callback your team can actually make. ## Cut the calls before they start

Every order status call you prevent is one less in the queue. Most of the fixes are in your own store:

- **Send tracking automatically** the moment an order ships, with the delivery estimate in the subject line.
- **Send a delay notice** before the customer notices the delay.
- **Make order lookup work for guests,** with an order number and email, no account needed.
- **Show realistic delivery dates at checkout,** and say clearly when an item is on backorder.
- **Send an out-for-delivery message** on the day it arrives.

The voice agent then handles the calls that still come in, at any hour.

## Questions to ask a vendor

- Which of our order and shipping systems can the agent read from, and how current is the data?
- How does it verify callers, and can we set what it shares before and after verification?
- Can it send tracking links by text and email, only to contact details on the order?
- How does it handle split shipments, backorders and gift orders?
- How does it hand calls to our team, and do they get a summary?
- Can it take the next step on the same call, such as a cancellation, a change or a return, within rules we set?
