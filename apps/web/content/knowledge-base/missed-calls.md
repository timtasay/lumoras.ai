A missed call is usually a customer who wanted to give you money and couldn't reach you. In a small business, most missed calls happen at predictable moments: when everyone is with a customer, during a rush, after hours or when the only line is already busy. You can measure them from your phone logs, put a rough dollar figure on them with simple arithmetic, and fix most of them with a mix of process and automation.

## Why small businesses miss calls

The causes are almost always practical:

- **Hands are busy.** A stylist is mid-color, a technician is under a sink, a dental assistant is chairside.
- **Peaks collide.** The phone rings hardest when the counter is busiest: the dinner rush, Saturday morning, the lunch hour.
- **One line, one person.** A second caller gets a busy signal or voicemail while the first is on hold.
- **After hours.** People call when they have a free moment, which is often evenings and weekends. Trades get urgent calls at night.
- **Routing gaps.** A phone menu that dead-ends, a forwarded number that rings an empty office, a cell phone in a truck with no signal.

Each industry has its own pattern. Restaurants miss calls during dinner service. Salons miss calls whenever every chair is full. Clinics miss calls during morning check-in. Retail shops miss calls when the floor is busy and the phone is in the back.

## Measure before you fix

You can't fix what you can't see. Pull two to four weeks of call data:

1. **Export your call logs.** Most carriers and VoIP providers show answered, missed and voicemail calls by time.
2. **Count missed calls by hour and day.** You will usually find clusters.
3. **Count voicemails.** Compare messages left to calls missed. The gap is callers who hung up without saying anything.
4. **Look at repeat callers.** The same number calling three times in an hour is a customer who tried hard. Plenty of others try once and move on.
5. **Check your callbacks.** How many missed callers did you call back, how fast, and how many did you actually reach?

If you have no logs at all, keep a tally sheet by the phone for a week, or ask your carrier what reports your plan includes.

## What missed calls cost

Use your own numbers. The examples below are illustrations, and your rates will differ.

**A salon.** Say you miss 8 calls a week, and half of them are people who want to book. That's 4 potential bookings. If you'd normally book 3 out of every 4 callers who want an appointment, and the average ticket is $90, that is 3 × $90 = $270 a week. Over a month of about 4.3 weeks, it comes to roughly $1,160.

**A restaurant.** Say you miss 20 calls across Friday and Saturday dinner service, and 10 of them were takeout orders. At $40 an order, that's $400 a weekend, or about $1,720 a month.

**A home services company.** Say you miss 3 calls a week and one of them is a repair worth $350. That's about $1,500 a month, before counting the maintenance plan or replacement job that sometimes follows a repair.

**A clinic.** Say you miss 15 calls a week during morning check-in and 2 of them are new patients who book elsewhere. If a new patient's first visit is worth $200, that's $400 a week, or about $1,720 a month, before any follow-up visits.

A handful of missed calls, multiplied by a real ticket and a month, usually adds up to more than it feels like day to day. There is also a longer tail. A new client who books elsewhere may stay there for years, and a regular who can't get through twice may start booking somewhere easier.

## Why voicemail doesn't solve it

Voicemail feels like a safety net, and it has three gaps. First, many callers won't leave a message, especially new customers who have other options; compare your voicemails to your missed calls to see this in your own data. Second, messages that are left still need a callback, and it often happens hours later, after the caller has booked elsewhere or stopped picking up. Third, during your busiest hours nobody has time to listen to messages and return them.

## Ways to fix missed calls

### Staffing and schedules

Put someone on the phone during the peaks you found: a part-time front desk shift on Saturday morning, or a host who owns the phone during dinner. It's personal and it works. It is also the most expensive option per call, and it doesn't cover nights.

### A callback routine

If you keep voicemail, set a rule: every missed call gets a callback within a fixed window, such as 15 minutes during open hours, by a named person. Track it on a simple sheet. A fast callback recovers some of what voicemail loses.

### Missed-call text-back

Some phone systems can automatically text a caller whose call went unanswered: "Sorry we missed you. Reply here or book online." It's cheap and catches people who prefer to text. In the US, business texting generally requires registering your number for application-to-person messaging, and you should only send texts your consent and opt-out practices support.

### Answering service

A human team answers, takes messages and sometimes books from a script. It's good for a personal touch and for complicated messages. It's usually billed per minute or per call, and the result is still often a message for you to return. See [AI receptionist vs. answering service](/insights/ai-receptionist-vs-answering-service) for a side-by-side comparison.

### AI receptionist

An AI receptionist answers every call on the first ring, at any hour. With access to your calendar or menu, it books, reschedules and takes orders during the call, so a would-be missed call ends as a finished task. Sonorch, the Lumoras product for salons and spas, publishes a result of 70% fewer missed calls. Read [what an AI receptionist is](/knowledge-base/what-is-an-ai-receptionist) to see how it works and where it falls short.

### A mix

Many businesses combine these. Staff answer when they can. Conditional forwarding sends overflow and after-hours calls to an AI receptionist. Anything it can't finish becomes a summary and a callback task for the morning. ## Cut the calls you don't need

Some missed calls are questions your customers could have answered without phoning. Read a week of call notes or summaries and sort them by reason. Then close the easy gaps:

- **Hours and holidays.** Keep them current on your website, your map listings and your voicemail greeting. Holiday hours are the classic miss.
- **Booking.** If people call to book, offer online booking and put the link where they look first.
- **Order status.** If takeout or repair customers call to ask "is it ready?", send a text when it is. If online shoppers call to ask where their order is, send tracking the moment it ships. Our guide to [where is my order calls](/knowledge-base/where-is-my-order-calls) covers the rest.
- **Directions and parking.** Add them to confirmation texts.
- **Prices.** Publish starting prices for your most common services.

Every call you remove this way is one less that can ring out at the wrong moment. It also frees whoever answers the phone to spend time on the calls that matter: new customers, complicated bookings and problems that need a person.

## Choosing the right fix

Match the fix to when your calls go unanswered:

- **Mostly during peaks:** overflow forwarding to an AI receptionist, or a dedicated person for those hours.
- **Mostly after hours:** an AI receptionist or an after-hours answering service.
- **Because of a single line:** busy forwarding, or a phone setup with more lines.
- **Because calls need a specialist:** route them to the right person and set a fast callback rule.
- **Very low volume:** a callback routine and text-back may be all you need.

Then set the monthly cost of each option against the arithmetic above. If missed calls cost you around $1,000 a month and a fix costs a fraction of that, the decision is easy. Our breakdown of [AI receptionist cost](/insights/ai-receptionist-cost) explains how the pricing models work.

## Track whether it worked

After the fix goes in, measure the same things you measured before: missed calls by hour, callback times, and bookings or orders that came in by phone. Read call summaries to learn what people call about, then update your website and greeting so fewer calls are needed in the first place. Check again after a month and again after your next busy season.
