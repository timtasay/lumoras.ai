Good music for retail stores fits the people who shop there, changes with the time of day, and is licensed for business use. Build a few playlists by daypart, set the volume separately for each area of the store, and play it through a business music service instead of a personal streaming account. Then check it from the sales floor, at different hours, the way a customer hears it.

## Start with licensing

In the US, playing recorded music in a business is generally a public performance, and that usually requires a license. Those licenses typically come from performing rights organizations such as ASCAP, BMI, SESAC and GMR, or are arranged through a business music service that covers them for you.

A few points to keep in mind:

- Personal streaming subscriptions are typically licensed for personal, non-commercial use. Read the terms before you play one in a store.
- Business music services differ in what they cover. Ask any provider, in writing, which licenses are included and which you need to arrange yourself.
- Narrow exemptions exist for some small establishments in some situations. Do not assume one applies to you.
- Rules differ outside the US, where other collecting societies handle licensing.

This is general information, not legal advice. Confirm your situation with your provider or an attorney.

## Start with who walks in

Your own taste is a poor guide. Picture your typical customer on a typical afternoon and ask what they would expect to hear in your store. A boutique selling linen and ceramics calls for something different from a sneaker shop or a hardware store.

Three questions narrow it down:

1. **How long should people stay?** Browsing stores benefit from calmer, slower music. Grab-and-go counters can run brighter.
2. **Do people need to talk?** Anywhere staff help customers, such as fitting rooms, a service desk or a pharmacy counter, keep vocals sparse and volume low.
3. **What should the store feel like?** Pick three words, like "warm, unhurried, local," and reject any track that fights them.

## Plan playlists by daypart

A store at 9 AM is a different place from the same store at 5:30 PM. Dayparting means switching playlists as traffic and mood change.

- **Open.** Gentle and steady while staff restock and the first customers arrive.
- **Midday.** A notch more energy as traffic builds.
- **Rush.** Upbeat, but not so loud it drowns out conversation at the register.
- **Close.** Wind down. A clear change in the music helps signal the store is closing before anyone says it.

Tempo is the easiest lever. As a starting point, try slower tracks in browsing hours and a moderately faster set at peak, then adjust by ear. There is no single correct number.

## Set zones and volume

Most stores have areas that need different sound:

- **Entrance:** brighter, to draw people in.
- **Sales floor:** the main mix.
- **Fitting rooms:** softer and calmer.
- **Checkout:** low enough for staff and customers to talk without raising their voices.

If your system supports zones, give each its own playlist and level. If it does not, set the single volume for the quietest area that matters, usually the checkout.

## Example: how fast a playlist repeats

Customers rarely notice repeats. Staff always do. Here is the arithmetic, as an example:

- A playlist of 40 tracks at about 3.5 minutes each runs 40 × 3.5 = 140 minutes.
- A 9-hour shift is 540 minutes.
- 540 ÷ 140 is about 3.9, so staff hear every song nearly four times a shift.

For each song to play only once in a 9-hour shift, you need about 540 ÷ 3.5, or roughly 155 tracks in rotation. Separate sets for each daypart get you there faster, since each set only plays for part of the day.

## Leave room for announcements

Music is one part of how your store sounds. Closing reminders, curbside pickup calls and staff pages all compete with it. - Confirm in writing what your current music source is licensed for.
- Write down three words for how your store should feel.
- Walk the store at opening, midday and close, and note what you hear in each area.
- Count your playlist length and work out how often it repeats per shift.
- Set a lower level at checkout and fitting rooms.
