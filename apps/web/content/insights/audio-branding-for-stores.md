Audio branding is the set of sounds people connect with your business: the style of music in the store, a short signature sound, the voice that makes announcements, and the way your phone greets callers. For a store, it means choosing those sounds on purpose and keeping them the same in every location and every channel. You already have an audio brand. The question is whether anyone chose it.

## The pieces of an audio brand

Most stores have five, whether they planned them or not.

1. **Music identity.** The genres, tempo, era and amount of vocals you play. Your guide to [music for retail stores](/insights/music-for-retail-stores) sets the practical rules; audio branding sets the taste behind them.
2. **Signature sound.** A short sequence of notes, a few seconds long, that opens announcements, plays at the end of a video, or sounds when the store opens. Sometimes called a sonic logo.
3. **Announcement voice.** The voice, pace and wording of store messages: closing time, curbside pickup, promotions, staff pages.
4. **Phone voice.** The greeting, hold experience and the voice of whoever, or whatever, answers.
5. **Ambient level.** How loud the store is overall, and how that changes by area and time of day.

When these match, the store feels deliberate. When they clash, customers notice, even if they cannot say why.

## Start from three words

Every audio choice gets easier with a short brief. Pick three words that describe how your brand should feel, then translate each into sound.

For example, a home goods store that wants to feel **warm, unhurried and local** might choose:

- **Music:** acoustic and soul, moderate tempo, light vocals, some local artists.
- **Signature sound:** three soft notes on a piano or marimba.
- **Voice:** a calm, friendly speaker at a relaxed pace.
- **Level:** quiet enough to talk at the register without leaning in.

A sporting goods store that wants **bright, quick and confident** would land somewhere very different. Neither is better. Each just has to match the brand.

## Write a one-page sound guide

Put your decisions on a single page so every manager and vendor works from the same reference:

- The three brand words
- Music: genres to use, genres to avoid, tempo range by daypart, explicit lyrics policy
- Signature sound: the audio file and where it plays
- Voice: who records announcements or which synthetic voice you use, plus speaking pace
- Scripts: approved wording for routine announcements
- Volume: target levels by zone and time of day

Keep it short enough that people actually read it.

## Announcements are part of the brand

Announcements are the part of store audio people consciously hear, so they carry the most weight. A few rules keep them on brand:

- Open with the signature sound so people know a message is coming.
- Keep each one under about 15 seconds.
- Duck the music underneath, then bring it back.
- Use the same voice and phrasing at every location.

Watch how often they play. Here is the arithmetic, as an example: a curbside announcement every 20 minutes over a 10-hour day plays 30 times. Customers may hear it once. Staff hear all 30, so write it to survive that. Our guide to [overhead paging and store announcements](/knowledge-base/overhead-paging-and-store-announcements) covers scheduling and zones.

## The phone counts too

For many customers, the first sound of your brand is the phone. A greeting that sounds rushed, or a long ring that ends in voicemail, undoes a lot of careful store design. If an AI receptionist answers for you, pick its voice and greeting with the same brief: same tone, same pace, same name for the business. Our explainer on [how voice AI works](/knowledge-base/how-voice-ai-works) covers what you can tune.

## Keep every location consistent

Consistency is where audio branding usually breaks. One store plays the approved set, another plays the radio, a third has the volume at full during a quiet morning. Scheduling music, announcements and levels centrally, with limited local control, fixes most of it.

## Licensing your signature sound and music

A signature sound you commission should come with clear rights to use it in stores, ads and phone systems. Get that in writing. Any recorded music you play still needs business licensing. In the US that generally means public-performance licensing through performing rights organizations or a licensed business music service. Confirm the details with your provider.

## How to tell if it is working

Store sound is hard to tie to sales, so do not trust anyone who promises a precise lift. Use practical checks instead:

- Walk each store at three times of day and compare what you hear to the guide.
- Ask staff which announcements or songs they are tired of.
- Note customer comments about the store being too loud or too quiet.
- Listen to five recorded phone calls and check the greeting matches.
