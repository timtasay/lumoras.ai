A no-show policy that works has three parts: a clear notice window, a consequence you will actually apply, and an easy way for clients to cancel in time. Put it in writing at booking, get the client to agree to it, and apply it the same way every time. A policy you waive for everyone is a sign on the wall and nothing more.

## Start with your own numbers

Before you choose a fee, find out what no-shows cost you. Here is the arithmetic, as an example:

- 3 no-shows a week
- Average ticket of $80
- 3 × $80 = $240 a week
- $240 × 52 weeks = $12,480 a year

Some of those slots you would have filled anyway, and some clients would have rebooked. Even so, a number like this tells you whether a deposit is worth the friction. Pull a month of no-shows and late cancellations from your booking system. If your system cannot report them, that is worth fixing first.

## Choose the notice window

The window should match how long you need to refill a slot.

- **Salons and spas:** 24 hours is common. For long services, like color or extensions, 48 hours gives you a real chance to fill the chair.
- **Clinics:** 24 to 48 hours, depending on how far out your schedule is booked.
- **Restaurants:** a few hours for small tables. Large parties and set menus often need a day or more.
- **Home services:** the business day before, since routes are planned the evening prior.

Shorter windows are friendlier. Longer windows protect more revenue. Pick the shortest one that still lets you refill.

## Pick a consequence you will apply

### Deposit at booking

The client pays part of the price up front, and it applies to the service. This works well for long, expensive or first-time appointments. It also filters out casual bookings. The downside is a little more friction at booking.

### Card on file with a fee

The client saves a card when they book and agrees that a no-show or late cancellation will be charged a set fee. This is less friction than a deposit. It only works if you are willing to run the charge, and if your POS stores the card through the payment processor so you never handle card numbers yourself.

### Card hold: worth it?

A card hold, or authorization, reserves an amount on the client's card without charging it. It sounds tidy, but holds typically expire after a period your processor sets, and an appointment booked weeks out may outlast it. Ask your processor how long holds last before you build a policy on them.

### Prepay for some bookings only

You can require prepayment only for certain services, peak times, or clients with past no-shows. This keeps booking easy for your reliable regulars.

### A note for clinics

Fees for missed medical or dental visits may be limited by insurance contracts, payer rules or local regulation. Check yours before you charge.

## Write it in plain language

Short and specific beats long and legal. Here is sample wording to adapt:

> We hold your time just for you. Please give us at least 24 hours' notice to cancel or reschedule. Cancellations with less notice, and missed appointments, are charged [50% of the service price / a $25 fee] to the card on file. Running late? Text us. We'll do our best to fit you in, and may need to shorten your service.

Put it on your booking page, in the confirmation text, and read it out when someone books by phone. ## Make it easy to cancel in time

Many no-shows come from people who simply forgot. A few habits keep them from becoming lost revenue:

- **Two-way reminders.** A text 24 to 48 hours ahead with a reply option. See our [appointment reminder text templates](/insights/appointment-reminder-texts).
- **An answered phone.** If clients call to cancel at 9 PM and reach voicemail, you find out too late. Our guide to [missed calls](/knowledge-base/missed-calls) shows how often this happens.
- **A waitlist.** A cancellation with notice is only good news if someone can take the slot.

## Apply it the same way every time

Consistency is what makes a policy enforceable. A few rules help:

1. **One grace per client.** Waive the first miss, note it on the client record, and say so kindly. The second time, the policy applies.
2. **Same rules for every staff member.** Clients notice when one stylist charges and another does not.
3. **Charge promptly.** A fee charged the same day feels like policy. One charged two weeks later feels like a surprise.
4. **Track repeat offenders.** After two misses, require a deposit for future bookings.

## What to do this week

- Count last month's no-shows and late cancellations and run the arithmetic above.
- Choose your notice window and one consequence.
- Write the policy in four sentences or fewer.
- Add it to your booking page, confirmations and phone script.
- Turn on two-way reminders and a waitlist.
- Decide your grace rule and tell your team.
